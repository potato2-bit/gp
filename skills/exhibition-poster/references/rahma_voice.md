# Rahma's voice — the humanizer pass for posters and pitches

Every line that ships goes through this. It's the project's standing humanizer mandate plus
the specific way Rahma writes: plain, warm, confident, and concrete. The reference sample is
her own competition text in `exhibition/COMPETITION_FORM_RESPONSES.md` — match that register.

## The sound of it

Read her own line out loud and copy the rhythm:

> "We built a robot that cleans the panels and makes its own cleaning water out of the air,
> so no water has to be brought in and nobody has to climb onto the roof."

> "You no longer have to choose between clean panels and saved water, because the robot makes
> its own."

Short sentences. Cause-and-effect joined by "so" and "because." A real person explaining a
clever thing to a smart friend who isn't an engineer. Never a brochure.

## Do

- **Lead with the human consequence, then the mechanism.** "Nobody climbs onto a hot roof —
  the robot drives itself." Not "an autonomous traversal subsystem eliminates manual intervention."
- **Attach a number to every claim.** "Saves ~37,674 m³ of trucked water a year" beats
  "saves significant water."
- **Use plain words.** water, dust, roof, air, clean — not "soiling mitigation via aqueous
  condensate." Keep one technical term per idea, and only when it earns its place (Peltier, AWH).
- **Say what's new as a contrast.** "Other cleaners need water trucked in. Ours makes its own."
- **Write headlines that state the takeaway**, so a slide makes its point even if nobody reads
  the bullets.
- **Keep her confidence.** The project works; say so plainly. No "we hope", "we aim to maybe".

## Don't (the AI tells to strip)

- **Significance inflation:** "revolutionary", "groundbreaking", "cutting-edge", "game-changing",
  "stands as a testament", "plays a vital role." Delete and state the fact.
- **Rule-of-three padding:** "efficient, scalable, and sustainable." Pick the one that's true
  and earned; drop the cadence-filler.
- **Vague attributions:** "studies show", "experts agree", "it is widely known." Name the source
  or cut it.
- **Hedging stacks:** "may potentially help to possibly reduce." Say what it does.
- **Copula avoidance / -ing fog:** "leveraging X to drive Y, enabling Z." Use a verb: "X does Y."
- **Em-dash overuse and the "not just X, but Y" template.** Use them once if at all.
- **Empty abstractions:** "various stakeholders", "a range of benefits", "innovative solution."
  Name the stakeholder, list the benefit, show the innovation.

## Poster-specific voice

- Title = what it is. Hook = why it's clever. Keep them separate and short.
- Big numbers carry the message; words only frame them.
- Each card is one idea with a verb-first headline ("Makes its own water", "Keeps workers off
  the roof", "Scales from plants to street-lamps").

## Deck-specific voice

- One idea per slide. The slide title is the sentence you'd say; the bullets are the proof.
- ≤ ~20 words of body per slide where possible. If you'd read it aloud verbatim, it's too long.
- End on the ask / next step, not a "thank you" slide with nothing on it.

## The quick test before shipping

1. Would a non-engineer understand every line? (Rubric rewards this directly.)
2. Does every claim have a number or a named source?
3. Did I cut every word that's there for rhythm, not meaning?
4. Does it sound like Rahma explaining it, or like a template? If template — rewrite.
