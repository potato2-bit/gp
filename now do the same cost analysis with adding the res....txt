AWH System Build and Cost Plan for the Egyptian Market
This document provides a detailed cost analysis and conceptual build guide for a 1 m² sorption-based AWH unit. It explains the function of each component, justifies the material choices based on a lowest-cost vs. optimum-performance strategy, and links these decisions to supporting scientific and market resources.
1. Detailed Cost & Component Analysis
This section breaks down the system into its core components, analyzing the choices for both a minimal budget and an optimized build.
Component 1: Sorbent Material (The Engine of the System)
* Importance: This is the core component that actively captures water molecules from the air. Its performance dictates the system's maximum potential water yield.
* Lowest Cost Option: CaCl₂ in Crushed Clay
   * Strategy: Use 2 kg of Calcium Chloride (CaCl₂) hosted in locally sourced, crushed clay or pottery shards.
   * Cost: ~250 EGP [3, 4]
   * Justification: Calcium Chloride is a powerful, low-cost hygroscopic salt [1]. Using free, locally sourced clay as a porous host prevents the CaCl₂ from turning into a corrosive liquid, making it a functional, bare-bones choice.
* Optimum Performance Option: CaCl₂ in Silica Gel
   * Strategy: Use 2 kg of CaCl₂ hosted in bulk silica gel.
   * Cost: ~500 EGP [3, 4]
   * Justification: Scientific reviews explicitly confirm that composite sorbents using silica gel and hygroscopic salts are a highly effective and well-researched method [2]. Silica gel's high internal surface area allows for far more efficient air contact and water capture than simple clay, justifying the extra cost for a higher yield.
Component 2: Enclosure/Chamber (The Insulated Reactor)
* Importance: The enclosure has two critical jobs: 1) Seal the system to trap water vapor during the day. 2) Act as a solar oven to efficiently "bake" the water out of the sorbent.
* Lowest Cost Option: Salvaged Wood Frame
   * Strategy: Build a frame from salvaged or low-grade wood with basic sealing.
   * Cost: ~800 EGP [4]
   * Justification: This meets the basic structural need for an insulated box, a concept central to solar-regenerated systems [5, 6]. The focus is on function over durability.
* Optimum Performance Option: Insulated, Well-Constructed Frame
   * Strategy: Use quality wood, properly sealed, and line the interior with rigid foam insulation.
   * Cost: ~2,000 EGP [4]
   * Justification: Superior insulation minimizes heat loss, allowing the sorbent to get hotter and release its captured water more completely. This investment directly translates to a higher daily water yield.
Component 3: Condenser Surface (The Water Collector)
* Importance: This is where the hot, water-rich vapor released from the sorbent turns back into liquid water for collection.
* Lowest Cost Option: Thin Aluminum Sheet
   * Strategy: Use a basic, thin-gauge aluminum sheet.
   * Cost: ~400 EGP [4]
   * Justification: Aluminum offers good thermal conductivity at a low cost in the Egyptian market [4], providing the minimum viable surface for condensation.
* Optimum Performance Option: Aluminum Sheet with Heat Sinks
   * Strategy: Use a thicker aluminum plate with heat sinks attached to the back.
   * Cost: ~900 EGP [4]
   * Justification: Heat sinks dramatically increase the effective surface area for cooling. This allows vapor to condense into liquid water much faster, maximizing collection before temperatures change.
Component 4: Glazing (The Solar Window)
* Importance: The glazing allows sunlight to enter while trapping heat inside (the greenhouse effect), which is essential for heating the sorbent.
* Lowest Cost Option: Standard Window Glass
   * Strategy: Use locally sourced, standard window glass.
   * Cost: ~600 EGP [4]
   * Justification: Glass provides the necessary transparency at the lowest possible price. However, it is heavy, fragile, and prone to cracking from thermal stress.
* Optimum Performance Option: Polycarbonate Sheet
   * Strategy: Use a multi-wall polycarbonate sheet.
   * Cost: ~1,500 EGP [4]
   * Justification: Polycarbonate is a superior engineering material. It's lightweight, nearly unbreakable, and offers better insulation than single-pane glass. Its durability and safety provide a much better long-term value.
Component 5: Airflow System (The Breathing Mechanism)
* Importance: During the night, fresh, humid air must be continuously circulated over the sorbent to "recharge" it with water.
* Lowest Cost Option: Passive Convection
   * Strategy: Use vents and rely on natural air currents.
   * Cost: 0 EGP
   * Justification: This zero-cost approach is functional but inefficient. The sorbent will only capture water from its immediate vicinity, severely limiting the nightly uptake.
* Optimum Performance Option: Active Fan-Forced Airflow
   * Strategy: Use two small 12V DC fans powered by a dedicated solar panel and small battery.
   * Cost: ~300 EGP [4]
   * Justification: Actively forcing air through the sorbent tray is a massive performance upgrade. It ensures the entire sorbent mass is constantly exposed to fresh, humid air, dramatically increasing the amount of water captured.

	3. How to Build the System: Conceptual Design
Think of the AWH unit as a "Solar Sorbent Box," a concept validated by research into solar-regenerated desiccant systems [5]. The structure is very similar to a solar oven [6] or a DIY solar water heater [7].
Step 1: Construct the Insulated Box (Enclosure)
* Build a shallow wooden box (e.g., 1.2m x 1.2m outer, 1m x 1m inner, 20 cm deep).
* Line the inner walls and floor with a rigid foam insulation board.
* Paint the entire interior, including the insulation, with matte black, high-temperature paint. This is crucial for absorbing maximum solar energy.
Step 2: Create the Sorbent Tray
* Build a shallow tray out of a thin aluminum sheet that fits inside the box, leaving a small gap around the edges.
* Drill many small holes through the bottom of the tray to allow for airflow.
* Paint this tray matte black as well.
* Prepare your sorbent composite (mix CaCl₂ with your host material, like silica gel or crushed clay) and spread it evenly in this tray.
Step 3: Install the Condenser and Collection System
* Take your main condenser aluminum sheet. Mount it inside the box, underneath the sorbent tray, at a steep angle (e.g., 45 degrees).
* The highest point of the condenser should be just below the sorbent tray, and the lowest point should end in a collection channel or gutter.
* Drill a small hole at the lowest point of the box and attach a tube to drain the collected water from the gutter to an external container.
Step 4: Add Airflow and Seal the Unit
* Cut two holes in the sides of the main box, one for intake and one for exhaust. Mount your small 12V DC fans here. One fan will pull air in, and the other will push it out, forcing it to flow through the holes in the sorbent tray.
* Place the sorbent tray on supports inside the box.
* Mount the polycarbonate or glass sheet on top of the box, using a high-quality silicone sealant to make it completely airtight. This is the glazing.
The Day/Night Operational Cycle:
* Night (Sorption Phase): The top of the box is cool. The fans are turned on (powered by a small battery charged during the day). The fans pull the cooler, more humid night air through the sorbent material for several hours, allowing it to capture water.
* Day (Desorption Phase): The fans are turned off. The box is completely sealed. The sun heats the interior through the glazing. The black surfaces get very hot, heating the sorbent and forcing it to release its captured water as hot vapor. This vapor fills the box and condenses into pure liquid water when it comes into contact with the cooler, angled condenser plate below. The pure water drips down the plate, into the gutter, and out the collection tube.
